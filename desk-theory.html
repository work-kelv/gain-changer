Theory for the Sound Desk

The music theory a sound engineer actually uses: note names and their frequencies, tempo and delay times, keys and chord numbers, and how a song is built so you know what is coming next. Each topic ends with what it means at the desk.

Notes & frequency

Letters A to G repeat every octave. A note's number says which octave it is in, and the number changes at C: middle C is C4, and the B just below it is B3 (Open Music Theory). An octave up doubles the frequency, so the same note name sits at 55, 110, 220 and 440 Hz.

Octave

Lowest notes (fundamentals)

Frequencies use standard tuning (A4 = 440 Hz, equal temperament). They and the lowest notes are standard figures, not from the books.

At the desk

  • Musicians say "the low E is booming"; the EQ wants a frequency. Tap the note to find it. A bass's low E is about 41 Hz; a guitar's low E is an octave up at about 82 Hz.
  • An octave is double the frequency. EQ widths are often measured in octaves, so a one-octave band at 200 Hz spans roughly 140–280 Hz.
  • Each note also has harmonics above its fundamental. That's why a bass still sounds on small speakers that can't play 41 Hz: you hear its upper harmonics.
  • Kick and bass share the lowest octave. The mixing books give that range to one of them (Savage: kick owns 60–80 Hz; Senior keeps sub-100 Hz on the main bass part). Savage, Senior

Tempo & delay times

The beat is the steady pulse you tap your foot to; tempo is how many beats per minute (BPM). In 4/4 there are four beats to a bar, counted 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 +, with sixteenths counted 1 e + a. In 6/8 the beat splits into three: two beats of three eighths, counted 1-la-li 2-la-li (Open Music Theory).

Formula: quarter note (ms) = 60,000 ÷ BPM. Halve it for each shorter note, ×1.5 for dotted, ×0.667 for triplets. (Owsinski)

At the desk

  • Delays timed to the tempo add depth without being noticed; they pulse with the music. Delays not in time stick out. Owsinski
  • If a quarter-note delay pushes the vocal too far back, try the eighth. Owsinski
  • Time a reverb to the song: feed it from the snare and set the decay so it just dies away by the next snare hit. Owsinski
  • Snare compressor release: let the level recover to 90–100% before the next backbeat (beats 2 and 4). Owsinski
  • Very short timed delays (8–16 ms) make a sound bigger or wider without being heard as echoes. Owsinski
  • Tap along with the drummer's hi-hat or the worship leader's strum for a few bars. The average settles quickly.

Keys & chord numbers

A key is the home note and the seven-note scale built on it. Number the scale's notes 1 to 7 and build a chord on each: the Roman numerals I to vii. Uppercase is major, lowercase is minor, and ° is diminished. In every major key the pattern is the same: I, IV and V major; ii, iii and vi minor; vii° diminished (Open Music Theory). That's why bands can call chords by number and change key without rewriting anything.

At the desk

  • The home chord's root (the I) is the note the bass returns to most. In a church room, that low note and its octave are the ones most likely to boom.
  • Chords move tonic → predominant → dominant → tonic. A V chord sounds like it needs to resolve, so a song rarely ends on it. Open Music Theory
  • The relative minor shares the same notes as the major key: A minor with C major, E minor with G major. A song that "sounds sad" may still be in the same key on the chart. Open Music Theory
  • When the band changes key for the last chorus (modulation), every resonance you notched moves with it. Listen again.
  • Most worship and pop songs loop four chords. Once you hear the loop, you know when the next section will turn over.

Song form

Songs are built from sections, usually 8 to 24 bars each. Verse, prechorus, chorus and postchorus always come in that order when they appear, and the cycle repeats. The bridge usually comes once, before the last chorus (Open Music Theory). Tap a section.

How to hear a section change coming

Drum fillThe usual signal that a new section starts on the next downbeat.
Backing vocals come inA strong sign of the chorus. Have the BV faders ready.
Rising register, more energyThe prechorus builds: shorter phrases, chords changing faster, leaving the home chord.
Lyrics repeatA chorus keeps the same words each time; verses change them. The title line is usually in the chorus.
Instruments drop outThinning out is part of how songs signal the end, along with repeating the last chorus.

Endings

A cadence is the chord move at the end of a phrase. Knowing the difference between an open and a closed one tells you whether the song is finishing (Open Music Theory).
