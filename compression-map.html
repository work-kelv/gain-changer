The controls

What each knob does, in the books' words.

Threshold

The level where compression starts. It has to actually be crossed, or nothing happens (Benediktsson). Some units set the amount with Peak Reduction or Input Gain instead; Senior warns that input-gain designs raise the level as they compress, which tempts over-processing.

Ratio

How hard it pushes back. 4:1 means 4 dB over the threshold comes out as 1 dB over (Owsinski). At 2:1, 6 dB over gives 3 dB of gain reduction (Oltheten). 10:1 or more is limiting (Owsinski, Benediktsson).

Attack

How long it waits before reacting. A slower attack lets the transient through (Benediktsson). Owsinski: a fast attack on punchy sources kills the transient.

Release

How long it takes to let go. Too slow on rhythmic parts and it never resets between hits and pumps (Benediktsson). Owsinski: the level should be back to 90–100% by the next beat.

Knee

Soft knee starts compressing gently below the threshold for a more natural onset; hard knee is more obvious, which is sometimes what you want (Senior).

Makeup gain

Turns the level back up after compression. Senior: set it by hand, because auto makeup makes the result sound louder and you overdo it. Benediktsson: match the bypassed level with the output, not the input, or you move the threshold.

Gain-reduction meter

Shows how much and how fast the compressor is working (Senior). Benediktsson watches it reset between hits to time the release.

Peak or RMS detection

Peak reacts to any overshoot; RMS follows the average, so it's smoother and closer to how the ear hears, and lets peaks through (Clarke, Senior).

Mix / dry knob

Blends the uncompressed signal back in: parallel compression built into one plug-in (Benediktsson).

Side-chain filter

Changes what the compressor listens to. A high-pass around 125 Hz on mid-range sources stops inaudible lows triggering it (Clarke); a low cut stops pumping (Senior); a ~2 kHz boost makes it catch piercing vocal notes (Oltheten).

Gating, expansion and limiting

The same box of controls, used three other ways. On the dynamics graph, a compressor bends the line down above the threshold; a limiter flattens it; an expander or gate bends it down below the threshold.

Expander

Turns quiet sound down further, below the threshold. Senior: a ratio of 1:1 does nothing, 4:1 is dramatic, ∞:1 is a gate. Use it for spill, noise and tails, before the compressor.

Gate

An expander at ∞:1: below the threshold the sound shuts off, or drops by the Range amount. Owsinski uses it for amp noise between guitar phrases, tom leakage, and a floppy kick's ring.

Gate controls
  • Attack: how fast it opens. Release: how fast it closes (Senior notes the naming is reversed from a compressor's).
  • Hold: a few ms keeps it open and stops chatter.
  • Hysteresis: separate open and close levels; leave at 0 dB and raise only until chatter stops.
  • Range: how far it turns down; less than full is like parallel processing.
  • Lookahead: a few ms saves the drum transient.
Gating drums
  • Clarke: gate kick, snare, toms and percussion (not overheads or room) as the first plug-in. Set the threshold first, very fast attack, and a decay that doesn't stop hits abruptly.
  • Owsinski: a 10–20 dB range, not fully off, unless it's an effect.
  • Valeriote: fastest attack; release from a couple of hundred ms to 1 s, shorter when the bleed or ring is bad.
  • Senior: set the threshold as high as still catches every hit.
Keying and ducking

Senior: let one track's level control another's processor. Bass ducks about 2 dB on each kick; guitars duck 1–2 dB under the vocal; delays and reverbs duck under the vocal so they bloom between phrases. Senior prefers a ducker (the same dip whenever the key is present) to a keyed compressor, which ducks most when the vocal is loudest.

Limiter

Ratio 10:1 or more (Owsinski). Rarely used in mixing except on bass (Owsinski); on a track Oltheten uses one only for clicky sources such as close piano, tambourine or congas. Senior lists its side effects: pumping, bass distortion, drums ducking.

Mix limiter

Savage: a brickwall limiter at the agreed level for review copies, taken off for the bounce that goes to mastering. Savage's five levels of gain reduction at the peaks: ≤1, 2–3, 4–6, 7–9, ≥10 dB; Savage prefers 2–3. Ceiling −0.2 dBFS. Valeriote: over-limiting makes a record tiring by the fifth or sixth song.

Track limiters

Valeriote ends a vocal chain with a peak limiter taking 1–3 dB (up to 6). Nyquist adds a limiter after the drum-bus compressor when the drums hit the master limiter more than 4 dB before everything else: 0–1 ms attack, 1–5 ms release, 1–6 dB.

Setting one up

Senior: two controls first
  • Rebuild the balance from the most important part down.
  • On a track whose fader won't settle, insert a compressor and lower the threshold until there's at least 6 dB of gain reduction.
  • Then back off to the least that keeps the fader steady, and set makeup gain by hand.
  • To hear what attack and release are doing, exaggerate threshold and ratio for a moment, then dial back.
Owsinski: timing to the snare
  • Start with the slowest attack and fastest release.
  • Speed up the attack until the snare starts to dull; stop there.
  • Set the release so the level is back to 90–100% by the next snare hit.
  • Bring the rest of the band back in and fine-tune.
Benediktsson: the workflow
  • Know why: correction (level the dynamics) or character (colour).
  • Pick a style, set threshold and ratio, shape with attack and release.
  • Judge in context, match the bypassed level, then rebalance faders and re-EQ what the compressor dulled.
Oltheten: check it honestly
  • Match loudness before comparing; 0.3 dB louder already fools you. Oltheten prefers slightly less makeup gain.
  • Play loud: no punch means less compression, a slower attack or a lower ratio.
  • Play quiet: if the lows vanish, compress more.

How much gain reduction

Nyquist keeps buses in a 0.5–2 dB range; past that, depth collapses. Benediktsson compresses a little at each stage: a few dB on groups, 3–4 dB for corrective levelling. Owsinski calls 6 dB or less control and 15–20 dB an effect. Savage: if a part needs more than about 10 dB, use two compressors in series; a lead vocal can take 15–18 dB in total at its peaks, about half while tracking and half in the mix.

Setups

Series

Two compressors, two jobs. Benediktsson: a fast FET catching only the peaks (hard knee, high ratio, very low threshold), then a slower opto riding the level (soft knee). Clarke: peak-detect then RMS-detect, 3–4 dB each. Senior: several gentle stages sound more transparent than one heavy one.

Parallel ("New York")

Blend a crushed copy under the dry signal. The dry keeps the transients; the copy adds density (Senior, Owsinski). Owsinski: 10 dB or more on the copy, EQ +6–10 dB at ~10 kHz and ~100 Hz, tuck it just under the dry. Senior: balance dry and wet from a group fader to avoid loudness bias.

Limiting

Ratio 10:1 or more (Owsinski). Rarely used in mixing except on bass (Owsinski). Oltheten uses a track limiter only on clicky transient sources such as close piano, tambourine or congas.

Gate / expander

The opposite job: turns quiet sound down. Owsinski uses it for amp noise, tom leakage and a floppy kick's ring, with the range at 10–20 dB rather than fully off. Senior puts it before the compressor, with a few ms of hold.

Group vs track

Per-track compression keeps parts steady against each other; group compression can't rein in one backing singer who gets loud (Senior). Keep loud, transient-rich tracks out of compressed groups (Clarke).

Order with EQ

Oltheten: big tonal EQ moves before the compressor, small corrections after. Andy Johns (in Owsinski): on bass, compressor first, then add the bottom with EQ. Andy Johns also warns that compression emphasises the strongest frequency range.

Common mistakes

A compressor on everything

Untweaked, or heavy at every stage (Benediktsson). Senior: over-compressing every channel "sucks the life out of a mix". Leave stable tracks alone.

Judging louder as better

A level jump on bypass fools you (Benediktsson, Oltheten). Match levels with the output control.

Fast attack on punchy parts

Kills the transient (Owsinski). On a strummy acoustic it kills the strum that cuts through (Benediktsson).

Release too slow

Pumps out of time with the music (Owsinski); the meter never resets (Benediktsson).

Asking it to do the fader's job

Compression alone won't hold a vocal in front. The last step is riding the fader line by line (Savage, Senior).

Audible bus compression

Oltheten: if you can clearly hear the bus compressor, fix the balance instead; it should be subconscious.

Where the books disagree

Acoustic guitar attack. Benediktsson uses a fast attack (but not so fast it kills the strum); Savage uses 50–100 ms on all struck and plucked sources.

Electric guitar. Senior and Savage usually don't compress it at all, because the amp already does. Owsinski starts at 8:1–10:1; Benediktsson at 2–3:1.

When to start the mix-bus compressor. Oltheten and Valeriote put it on early with fixed settings. Savage and Nyquist engage it late, so it doesn't hide balance problems.

How much on the mix bus. Nyquist about 0.5 dB; Owsinski 2–3 dB; Senior up to 2–3 dB; Savage 3–6 dB; Valeriote 2–4 dB at the end.

Not from the books. These are studio mixing books. In a live room, stage volume and the room change what you hear, so treat every number as a starting point.
